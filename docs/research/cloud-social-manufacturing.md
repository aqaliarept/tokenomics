# Manufacturing literature points our search upstream, not at routing

**Bottom line in plain English.** We asked whether the cloud manufacturing (CMfg) and social manufacturing (SocialM) literatures hold a task-allocation, service-composition or capacity/resilience method that fits our economy and could beat our best hand-written rules: MWW for ordering (each order goes whole to one producer, drawn in proportion to how much that producer can make right now) and `entity_planner` for producing. Pricing and incentive design were out of scope. **No method we found was ever tested against a rule as strong as MWW.** The papers beat greedy heuristics, genetic algorithms or other learned policies, mostly on synthetic instances. Nearly all of them assume things our economy forbids. They put a central platform in charge, or let agents negotiate in several rounds within one decision period, or treat providers as pure servers that never buy inputs or maintenance from each other. The handful of decentralised, quantitatively tested methods (trust-weighted partner choice, platform capacity matching, a learned ranking correction) act on signals that our recorded data already show to be weak or capped: agent history is inert, and better matching can recover at most about 4% of filled units. What the literature does offer is a set of *diagnostic frames*. Three of them aim at losses we have actually measured, and each can be checked offline on recorded data before anything is built. (1) **Upstream-supply-aware availability**, from multi-tier service composition: about three quarters of the consumer units MWW loses expire while the chosen producer waits for input products. (2) **Outage-aware re-routing**, from exception-triggered re-composition: MWW's weight ignores outages, and MWW loses visibly more units to outaged producers than round robin does. (3) **Bottleneck-first restoration**, from joint production–maintenance scheduling: it is the only family that speaks to the run-down damage that sank learned corrections on MWW, and the only one that could beat `entity_planner`. None of the three is a ready algorithm. All three are hypotheses until their checks come back.

Three structural mismatches recur across the whole matrix. First, **every conflict-avoidance mechanism in the decentralised CMfg work needs a channel our agents lack within a cycle**: award and reject messages, proposal rounds, or a central coordination agent at execution. Our producer auction plus TTL 2 already behaves like a contract net truncated to a single retry ([Liu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procir.2018.03.138); [Delaram 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.promfg.2021.10.043)). Second, **no CMfg or SocialM paper found models providers that must buy their own inputs and maintenance from peers**. In SCOS a provider never depends on another provider to stay able to serve, so the starvation cascades that ended our early PPO producers cannot arise in those models ([Li, Liu, Shi 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01795v1)). Third, **the field optimises the requester's QoS, not system fill**. In the 2024 review's count of 78 papers, cost appears as an indicator 68 times and time 66, while provider load balancing appears only 5 times ([Li, Liu, Shi 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01795v1)). More than half of CMfg scheduling papers are bio-inspired metaheuristics run by a central optimiser ([Halty 2020](http://www.aimspress.com/article/doi/10.3934/mbe.2020377)).

## Recorded data already caps the matching and history ideas

Before ranking the survivors, it is worth stating what our own experiments already rule out, because it removes most of the decentralised literature. The idea behind trust and reputation (approach 10) and behind learned history features is that a producer's past behaviour predicts its next acceptance. The ES main runs found the router's agent-local history **`history_inert`**: it adds nothing measurable. The learned router reached round robin's level but finished 59–83 units *below* MWW, beyond the noise floor ([EXP-0060](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0060-es-main/RESULT.md)). The decoder check's dispersion reading was **`history_flat`**, though borderline ([EXP-0057](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0057-decoder-check/RESULT.md)). Szaller's own result points the same way: the trust gain shrinks as load rises, and trust concentrates load on the reliable providers ([Szaller 2020](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/9871/1/Szaller_1_31011885_ny.pdf)), which is the herding pathway we already observe.

Better matching (approaches 2 and 11) is capped by the split-need re-test. Under MWWk at 12 producers, the units lost because an order did not fit, and that a different *choice* of producer could have saved, total **6,910 against 180,255 filled units, about 3.8%**. Units that only splitting could have saved come to 0.55% after completion correction, which gave the reading `whole_suffices` ([EXP-0066 reading.json](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0066-split-need/reading.json)). The 3.8% figure is our arithmetic on the recorded totals, not a registered reading. Finally, learned corrections in the style of approach 14 have already been tested on top of MWW. They tripped the run-down and starvation guards about ten times more often than MWW's own re-keying noise does: 542 of 1,000 member scores were disqualified. MWWk itself runs producers down on 0.80 of the training worlds and starves consumers on 0.97 ([DIAGNOSTICS 28](/srv/research/tokenomics/docs/DIAGNOSTICS.md)). Any new mechanism therefore has to act on something other than history, matching or a free-form residual.

**Preliminary local-data observations (unpaired arm means computed by the researchers from [EXP-0057 episodes.csv](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0057-decoder-check/artifacts/episodes.csv); not the pre-registered reading).** These are per-episode averages, with `entity_planner` as the producer.

## Three mechanisms aimed at measured losses, each with an offline check

The ranking below weighs three things: whether the mechanism targets a loss class we have measured, whether its fit to our domain can be adapted without breaking local information or whole-order routing, and how cheaply the check can be run. Under the project's root-cause rule, none of the three is a next strategy. Each is a candidate *diagnosed cause*, waiting for its check.

### 1. Upstream-supply-aware availability ("deep makeable"), from multi-tier composition

**Mechanism.** Correlation-aware and multi-tier SCOS value a provider by the state of the upstream services co-selected with it ([SCOS survey](https://www.peeref.com/works/5379635); [Qanbari 2014](https://dsg.tuwien.ac.at/Staff/sd/papers/Zeitschriftenartikel%20S.%20Qanbari%202014%20Toward%20Portable.pdf)). In our terms there are two local versions. One is a routing weight that credits an assembler for the inputs its eligible suppliers can make within TTL, not only for what is in its own stock. The other is producer-side: `entity_planner` buying inputs earlier, or from suppliers with stock. MWW's weight `makeable_qty_now` = min(capacity-makeable, input-stock-makeable), so it already looks one tier upstream through the assembler's own inventory ([metrics.py:83–127](/srv/research/tokenomics/tokenomics/tokenomics/domain/metrics.py)). The open question is whether a second tier adds anything.

**Why it could act on a real gap.** Input-short expiry is the single largest loss class: about 1.9 times MWW's filled units at 12 producers (preliminary table above). It is also the class on which MWW beats round robin. A second preliminary spot check, on 24,000 ES member episodes each paired with their MWWk start (A0), found that members which cut input-short losses gained units: the correlation between Δ input-short and Δ units was r = −0.35. But they converted only about 14 of every 61 units of input-short loss removed into extra filled units ([EXP-0070 members.csv](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0070-es-rebased/artifacts/a1-s0/members.csv) paired with [bar_episodes.csv](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0070-es-rebased/artifacts/bar_episodes.csv); rows share world-episodes, so they are not independent). Losses partly migrate between classes. That migration is exactly what the check must separate.

**Offline check.** *Stage A runs on existing rows, locally, in seconds.* From EXP-0057 `episodes.csv` and EXP-0070 `members.csv`, compute for each arm pair (MWW vs RR, TS vs MWWk, member vs A0) and each world-episode a **conversion rate**: Δ`units` ÷ −Δ`trace_ttl_lost_units_consumer_inputs_short`. Alongside it, compute the producer-side Δ`trace_ttl_lost_units_producer_inputs_short` and Δ`trace_lost_units_producer_ttl`, which show whether the assemblers' own input orders are dying. *Stage B needs a traced replay.* It would use a read-only observer modelled on `eval/split_need.py`, over EXP-0057's 71 world-episodes, MWWk arm, R = 2: about 142 jobs, a pod run well inside the auto-approved budget. For each consumer unit that expires input-short, it classifies the decision-time state as one of four:

- **Tier-1 fixable:** another eligible assembler had `complete_qty_now` ≥ the order.
- **Tier-2 reachable:** no assembler had stock, but eligible suppliers of the missing inputs had makeable quantity covering the shortfall.
- **Procurement lost:** the assembler's input order was placed and then lost.
- **Supply short:** nothing upstream could make it.

**Confirms:** a conversion rate clearly above zero, *and* tier-2-reachable plus procurement-lost units at or above 5% of filled consumer units at 12 or 42 producers (the same bar EXP-0066 used). Tier-2 dominance licenses a "deep makeable" router. Procurement-lost dominance makes `entity_planner`'s input buying the target, which is the one way this family could beat the producing rule. **Refutes:** a conversion rate near zero (losses only change class), tier-1-fixable dominance (the problem is MWW's draw variance, not depth), or supply-short dominance (capacity binds, which hands the question to mechanism #3).

### 2. Outage-aware re-routing, from exception-triggered re-composition

**Mechanism.** CMfg re-composition re-plans after a service fails, and an agent-based study found transfer to an external provider beat internal substitution ([Zhang & Ren 2023](https://aimspress.com/article/doi/10.3934/mbe.2023147); [Wang (BUAA)](https://research.buaa.edu.cn/en/publications/enhancing-reconfiguration-of-cloud-manufacturing-service-composit/)). The adaptable local version is a short failure memory: after a sub-order to producer p is rejected outright, the ordering agent down-weights p for a few cycles. The variant in which a producer publishes zero availability during its own outage breaks the domain rules. The outage reason is "derived from hidden shock state that no agent observes, so no policy … may read" it ([producing_agent.py:34–36](/srv/research/tokenomics/tokenomics/tokenomics/domain/producing_agent.py)). The memory must therefore key on the rejection itself, not its reason.

**Why it could act on a real gap.** I verified in code that `makeable_qty_now` has no outage term ([metrics.py:83–127](/srv/research/tokenomics/tokenomics/tokenomics/domain/metrics.py)), and no outage handling appears in `eval/routing_diagnostics.py`. An outaged producer keeps degrading but completes nothing, so its stock and free capacity can stay high. That plausibly makes it *more* attractive to MWW, an attractor effect that is an inference, not yet traced. A preliminary spot check on EXP-0057 rows supports the direction. Over all rows, consumer units rejected at outaged producers average **15.6 for MWW vs 3.6 for RR at 12 producers, and 108.2 vs 10.9 at 42**. On shocked worlds, MWW's outage-attributed loss (`trace_lost_units_consumer_outage` + `trace_lost_units_consumer_ttl_outage`) is 49.1 units per episode at 12 (2.3% of 2,105.9 filled) and 210.9 at 42 (4.4% of 4,820.7). The ceiling is small, but it is the one MWW blind spot confirmed in code. This family cannot help `entity_planner`.

**Offline check (existing rows, local).** Use EXP-0057 `episodes.csv`, shocked rows, and replicate on [EXP-0070 bar_episodes.csv](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0070-es-rebased/artifacts/bar_episodes.csv), which holds MWW and RR on 140 world-episodes. For each world-episode, compute the paired MWW − RR difference in outage-attributed consumer and producer loss (`trace_lost_units_{consumer,producer}_outage`, `…_ttl_outage`). Then parse `shock_schedule` for the outage windows and sum `fulfilled_trace` inside each window and for two cycles after it. Compare each world's difference with its per-world `noise_floor` in [atlas_v2.json](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0052-headroom-atlas-confirm/reclassified/atlas_v2.json) (93–149 units on the entries sampled). **Confirms:** MWW's excess outage loss exceeds the floor on most shocked 42-producer worlds, *and* the in-window fulfilled deficit matches it. That licenses one cheap rule, MWW plus a rejection memory, for a pod comparison against MWW. **Refutes:** a difference below the floor, or one offset by gains elsewhere in the same window. Then drop the idea.

### 3. Bottleneck-first restoration, from joint production–maintenance scheduling

**Mechanism.** The joint production–maintenance literature shows that optimising production alone sacrifices maintenance. It resolves this with a shared shadow price on the coupling constraint ([Rokhforoz & Fink 2020](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2011.13743)), or by maintaining the throughput bottleneck first and using slack elsewhere as free maintenance windows ([Zhou 2015](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JQME-09-2013-0059/full/html); [Durandt 2016](https://sajie.journals.ac.za/pub/article/view/1370)). Our coupling is sharper than theirs. Restoration products are made with *other* producers' capacity and compete with consumer orders in the same greedy auctions. Three hypotheses follow. **H1, crowd-out:** better consumer routing displaces restoration bids at the restoration suppliers. **H2, bottleneck neglect:** restoration lands off the binding layer. **H3, fund feedback:** routing reshapes cut-off prices, and so the fund shares that pay for restoration.

**Why it could act on a real gap.** Run-down is the binding failure of MWW-based policies: MWWk's run-down share is 0.80 and perturbations worsen it ([DIAGNOSTICS 28](/srv/research/tokenomics/docs/DIAGNOSTICS.md)). This family is also the only one that could beat `entity_planner`. **The first evidence leans against the simplest story, however.** In a preliminary spot check on the same 24,000 paired EXP-0070 member episodes, Δ`investment_rejection_rate` correlates *positively* with Δ(`r_end`/`r_start`) (r = +0.14), and so does Δ`trace_fund_share_herfindahl` (r = +0.17). H1 and H3 both predict negative signs at episode level. Episode aggregates may hide auction-level crowd-out, so this weakens the hypotheses but does not refute them.

**Offline check.** *Stage A runs on existing rows.* Repeat the paired correlations properly, aggregated to one value per world-episode with bootstrap intervals, on EXP-0070 `members.csv` against A0 and across EXP-0057's six arms. Use `investment_rejection_rate`, `trace_investment_rejection_rate`, `r_start`, `r_end`, `min_capacity_ratio` and `trace_fund_share_herfindahl_episode`. *Stage B needs a traced replay.* It would add a new eval-side hook, outside `domain/`, that logs each rejected investment bid together with the class of the bids accepted ahead of it (consumer, input or investment), plus per-producer, per-type capacity each cycle. It would run on DIAGNOSTICS 28's knife-edge worlds (12-0005, 12-0014, 12-0044, 12-0408, 12-0045) under MWWk and a guard-tripping member. **Confirms H1/H2:** a material share of rejected restoration bids lose to consumer bids at restoration-supplier auctions in the cycles before the guard trips, or restoration goes mostly to non-binding layers. That makes a producer-side restoration timing and sourcing rule a diagnosed candidate against `entity_planner`. Giving restoration auction priority would change a domain rule, which is a human gate. **Refutes:** restoration bids are rarely rejected, or lose mainly to other investment bids. Run-down is then degradation outrunning restoration supply, and the bottleneck-first rule targets the wrong lever.

**A cheap side check, not shortlisted.** The idea of simulation-driven rule *selection* ([Zhou 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/TII.2019.2894111)) suggests measuring how much a per-world oracle choice among {MWW, MWWk, RR} would gain over always-MWW on EXP-0057 rows, with each world's choice validated on its second episode to remove selection bias. The preliminary paired split between 12 and 42 producers suggests there may be some selector headroom. This is a portfolio over rules, not a better mechanism, so it ranks below the three above.

## Literature gaps that matter to us

The searches surfaced several absences, each from abstract-level searching rather than exhaustive review, so they count as weak evidence that the work is rare. **No paper evaluates purely local, one-shot, simultaneous routing without communication against a capacity-proportional randomised dispatcher.** **No CMfg, SocialM or production–maintenance paper models capacity that degrades every cycle and is restored by purchases from other network members.** Maintenance is always a local action, and capacity sharing and maintenance sit in separate literatures ([Geurtsen 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v305y2023i2p501-529.html) notes that integrating production, maintenance and resource scheduling is sparse). **No paper treats circular supply dependencies, or a router whose throughput gains cause run-down at another agent.** **No CMfg learning paper reports transfer across provider counts**; the largest GNN instance found had 20 locations ([Fomin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/access.2024.3522020)). **SocialM's complementary-community allocation exists only as concept**, and Szaller names interdependent, multi-resource orders as future work ([Szaller & Kádár 2021](https://eprints.sztaki.hu/10244/1/Szaller_407_32090056_ny.pdf)). Agent-based supply-chain formation and multi-agent RL for joint maintenance and production were outside this search and are the most likely places for closer analogues.

## Evidence quality caveats

**Only five sources were read in full text**: Szaller 2020, Szaller & Kádár 2021, Ghahramani 2026 (a preprint), Rokhforoz & Fink 2020 (a preprint; its claimed journal version is unverified), and the Li/Liu/Shi 2024 SCOS review (a preprint). Everything else is grade B (abstract) or C (snippet). Instance sizes, baselines and gains are missing for most CMfg papers. Several claims are explicitly **unverified**: Wang 2022's "beats six MARL algorithms" (snippet only); Zhang 2025's six uncertain-event types (snippet); the 32.8% / 28.6% DRL gains attributed to Wang 2024 (unresolved link, omitted above); the authorship of the "LSTM-DoubleDQN breakdown" paper ([ScienceGate](https://boch.sciencegate.app/works/W4392942263), mixed records); Kaihara 2017 (restricted); the per-paper mapping of the BUAA re-composition links; and the authorship of the 2018 IJAMT SCOS survey. Where the literature does report numbers, the comparisons are internal (metaheuristic vs metaheuristic, or cooperative vs non-cooperative variants of one model), mostly on random synthetic QoS data, and the field's own review flags the absence of a shared simulator ([Li, Liu, Shi 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01795v1)). The local-data figures in this report are of two kinds. The researchers' unpaired arm means are labelled as such. My paired spot checks have no noise floors, use non-independent rows in EXP-0070, and are **not** pre-registered readings. They indicate direction only.

## Conclusion

The literature's main value to us is negative and diagnostic. It confirms that the hard parts of our domain are the parts nobody has modelled: decentralised one-shot routing, maintenance bought from peers, and supply that is coupled and circular. It also explains why imported methods keep landing at round-robin level: they were never asked to beat an availability-proportional rule. The useful shift is from asking "which allocation algorithm" to asking "where do MWW's lost units actually die". The recorded traces already say expiry while inputs are short is three quarters of the loss, that outaged producers are a measurable blind spot, and that run-down is the guard that binds. Of the three checks, the Stage A analyses of mechanisms #1 and #2 run on existing CSVs in minutes and should come first. Their outcomes decide whether the next pod money goes to a traced supply replay, a one-line MWW amendment, or the producer side, where `entity_planner`, not MWW, is the rule left to beat.
