# Replaying identical worlds can replace the critic

Yes: the team can be improved by reinforcement learning without a learned critic, and the AlphaGo-style idea that "the candidate must beat the champion on the same game" carries over intact, because none of the parts this project needs depend on two players fighting each other. The strongest precedent is neural routing, not Go. Kool et al. trained a policy by comparing each sampled solution with a frozen "best model so far" on the *same* problem instance, swapped the champion only after a paired t-test, and found this beat a learned critic ([Kool et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.08475)). POMO then showed that a better gradient baseline is the mean of several of the learner's *own* rollouts on that instance ([Kwon et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16011)). This simulator is deterministic, seeded and copyable, so it supports both comparisons exactly, a property ordinary RL benchmarks lack. The literature also marks the limits. A single score per 100-cycle episode is too coarse: critic-free methods lost to PPO on long-horizon control when their groups shared nothing and their advantage was one number per episode ([de Oliveira et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03527)). Pairing rollouts on the same seed does not guarantee lower gradient variance ([Sakib 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24144)). And no published work trains 50-100 agents this way in a stateful economy, so the gain here has to be measured. The recommendation is a two-stage pilot of about 30-45 vCPU-h. Stage 0, a diagnostic of about 3-5 vCPU-h, measures the signal first. Stage 1 fine-tunes the imitated GNN team with a same-seed, per-cycle, leave-one-out baseline (each rollout compared with the average of its siblings), anchored to the imitation policy and gated against the frozen champion. A sampled-fork credit check targets the 42-producer drain. Evolution strategies and a centralized critic stay as measured fallbacks, not the main path.

## The AlphaZero loop survives without an opponent

**In plain terms.** AlphaGo Zero improved by playing, training a new network, and letting that network replace the old "best player" only if it won a test match. The question here is which parts of that loop need an adversary, and which work just as well when two teams play separate, identical copies of an economy and are compared afterwards.

The AlphaGo Zero loop has three parts. The current best player generates games by self-play with tree search. A network is trained on the search's move choices and on the game result. Then a gate decides promotion: the new player replaced the best one only "if it won by a margin of 55%" ([Silver et al., arXiv 1712.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.01815)). Reimplementations describe that match as **400 games at a 55% threshold** ([ELF OpenGo](https://www.scribd.com/document/617566873/ELF-OpenGo); [AlphaZero.jl](https://docs.juliahub.com/General/AlphaZero/stable/autodocs/)). KataGo requires **100 of 200** test wins ([Wu 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.10565)). Telling for this project, **AlphaZero dropped the gate** and "simply maintains a single neural network that is updated continually" ([arXiv 1712.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.01815)). Gating is a guard against regressions under a noisy evaluation, not the source of learning.

Only three things in that recipe need two-player zero-sum structure: the ±1 outcome defined against an opponent, the tree-search backup that flips sign between players, and the automatic curriculum in which "the opponent gets stronger as you do." The policy-improvement step, the gate and the idea of a value estimate are game-agnostic. The curriculum can be rebuilt without interaction by defining a "win" as "my team beat the frozen champion team on the same seeded world." Three precedents do exactly that. Kool et al. use as their REINFORCE baseline "the cost of a solution from a deterministic greedy rollout of the policy defined by the best model so far." They replace that baseline only when a one-sided paired t-test is significant at **α = 5% on 10,000 instances**, and they resample the evaluation instances after each replacement to avoid overfitting them. They explicitly note the "similarities with self-play improvement" ([Kool et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.08475)). GAZ PTP makes the framing literal. The learner and a frozen historical policy each solve identical copies of an instance, the learner wins if its result is at least as good, and the historical policy is replaced through an arena test. It cut the TSP-100 optimality gap from 27.15% to **3.16%**, although on one job-shop size the plain greedy baseline was as good ([Pirnay et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.04403)). Ranked Reward does the same against a percentile of the agent's own recent scores: a 75% threshold beat Gurobi on bin packing by **more than 6%** ([Laterre et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1807.01672)). In none of these do the two "players" interact. Only their outcomes on the same instance are compared.

The rest of the competitive toolkit buys nothing here. AlphaStar's league, PSRO and fictitious self-play exist because, against an adversary, an agent "may forget how to win against a previous version of itself" and cycles through rock-paper-scissors strategies ([DeepMind AlphaStar](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning/)). Two teams that never touch each other produce one transitive score per world, so there is nothing for exploiters or Elo ratings to fix. One warning from that literature does transfer, inside the team. PSRO measured that an independently trained partner can lose **34% of reward** when paired with a partner from another run, and this happened only where coordination mattered ([Lanctot et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00832)). In Hanabi, self-play reached 23.97 points, but pairing agents from different runs ("cross-play") scored **2.52** ([Hu et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979)). The producer and orderer policies are coordination partners, so candidates should be compared with the champion **as whole teams**, never by swapping one role, unless mixed teams are what will be deployed. Ranking roles by their average performance across varied partners also invites "relative overgeneralization": settling for robust-but-mediocre conventions ([Bucci & Pollack 2005](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gecco2005/docs/p539.pdf)).

A frozen champion has one well-documented weakness as the *gradient* baseline, as opposed to the gate. Once it is strong, the sampled policy rarely beats it, so advantages (how much better an action did than the baseline) are "usually negative," which raises gradient variance. POMO therefore replaced the greedy champion with the mean of N rollouts of the same instance ([Kwon et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.16011)). PTP also found that an improved sampled policy does not always yield an improved greedy policy, which can delay promotion ([Pirnay et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.04403)). The lesson: **use the champion for the gate and the curriculum, and use the learner's own same-seed siblings for the gradient.**

## Same-seed group baselines beat critics, on short decisions

**In plain terms.** A policy gradient pushes the policy toward actions that did better than expected. "Expected" comes from a *baseline*: a learned critic, or simply the average score of other attempts at the same problem. The family below (GRPO, RLOO, POMO, Kool's rollout baseline) uses the second option. It works very well when an "attempt" is effectively one decision. It weakens over long episodes unless the comparison is made per time step or from shared intermediate states.

GRPO samples a group of G answers to the same prompt and uses the group's mean (and standard deviation) as the baseline; DeepSeekMath used **G = 64** ([Shao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.03300v3)). Dividing by the group's standard deviation adds a bias that over-weights near-deterministic problems. Removing it gives Ã_i = R_i − mean(R), which is **RLOO up to a factor G/(G−1)** ([Liu et al. 2025, Dr. GRPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20783)). RLOO ("leave one out") compares each of k samples with the mean of the other k−1. In RLHF, k = 2-4 beat PPO-with-critic by roughly 10-32 win-rate points (TL;DR 77.9 vs 67.6; HH-Llama 64.1 vs 32.0). PPO's clipping fired on under 5% of samples there ([Ahmadian et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14740)). ReMax uses one greedy rollout as the baseline and saves about **46% GPU memory** ([Li et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10505)). On TSP20, Kool's rollout baseline reached a 0.34% gap against 0.96% for a critic and was "much more effective" ([Kool et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.08475)). POMO's same-instance mean reached **0.14%** on TSP100 with no critic network ([Kwon et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16011)). The pattern is consistent: when the comparison is on the *same input*, a sampled baseline matches or beats a learned critic.

The counter-evidence is just as specific. Run on Gymnasium control, with groups made of parallel episodes that had *independent* resets and one return per episode applied to every step, all critic-free variants performed "substantially below PPO" on Acrobot, MountainCarContinuous and HalfCheetah, because an episodic scalar "cannot distinguish transitions of different value within one trajectory." Groups of G = 8 beat 16-64, and γ ≈ 0.9-0.95 beat γ = 1 ([de Oliveira et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03527)). Two fixes recover most of the loss without a critic. GR2PO normalizes return-to-go (the reward collected from step t to the end) across parallel episodes *at the same time index*. That reached **84-96% of PPO** on Ant, Walker2d and Humanoid (Ant 6,335 vs 6,610), while plain GRPO collapsed (Ant 1,013) ([Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19850)). GiGPO builds step-level groups from states that recur across a group's trajectories and gained **12% on ALFWorld and 9% on WebShop** over GRPO at no extra rollout cost ([Feng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10978)).

The strongest fix, and the one this simulator uniquely allows, is the *vine* or *fork*: copy the state at cycle t, play several continuations, and use their mean as the value of that state. VinePPO does this with **K = 9** continuations. It costs 2-5× more per iteration than PPO but reaches PPO's peak in **1.5-3× less wall-clock**, and it diagnosed PPO's own critic as "near chance" at picking the best action despite 0.7-0.9 explained variance, with an error that grew with depth into the trajectory ([Kazemnejad et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01679)). TRPO's 2015 vine procedure added common random numbers (CRN: the same random draws reused across the branches being compared) so the branches differ only by the action ([Schulman et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.05477)). VinePPO calls the reset-to-any-state requirement rare in RL. Here it is already in use: `eval/bounded_rollout_orderer` and `teacher_audit` deep-copy the system per candidate (EXP-0042).

The general principle behind "same seed" is PEGASUS. Fixing the random-number sequences turns any stochastic problem into a deterministic one, so policy search optimizes a fixed function over a set of scenarios ([Ng & Jordan 2000](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3878)). In this project the episode seed fixes demand, drift, patterns and shocks, so grouping rollouts by world-seed *is* PEGASUS. It removes between-world variance, which the headroom atlas shows is very large (saturated, hard and collapse worlds side by side). The 2026 "Luck Is Not Skill" study is the needed caution. Pairing removes only the between-scenario part of the variance in reward differences and "need not reduce gradient variance." In a one-step counterexample, contrast variance fell 9% while gradient variance rose **2.2×**. In its agent simulator, **52-54% of paired groups carried a constant reward** (no signal) against about 13% unpaired, and the paired run showed no speed-up in steps or wall time ([Sakib 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24144)). The benefit also decays as histories diverge. In a coupled economy, where a routing change cascades through capacity, investment and death, candidate and sibling rollouts may decorrelate within tens of cycles. **The covariance has to be measured per cycle offset before it is relied on**, and zero-spread groups (saturated or collapsed worlds) should be dropped rather than divided by their standard deviation.

## Per-agent credit costs one fork per agent per decision

**In plain terms.** Even a perfect team-level baseline tells the team how much better *it* did, not which of 60-90 agents caused it. Each agent's gradient carries everyone else's noise. *Difference rewards* fix this by asking, for one agent, "how would the team have done had this agent done something default instead?" A forkable simulator can answer that exactly, at the price of one extra simulation per agent asked about.

A difference reward is D_i = G(z) − G(z with agent i's action replaced). It stays aligned with the team objective while becoming far more sensitive to agent i's own action ([Wolpert & Tumer 2001](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20010066523/downloads/20010066523.pdf)). In the Tumer group's domains it gave large gains. In air-traffic flow, personalized difference rewards cut congestion by **up to 45%** against agents using the global reward ([Tumer & Agogino 2007](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas07/html/pdf/AAMAS07_0453_bd798e682c64b239665c2e4d0efcd1df.pdf)), and the group reports results scaling to 1,000-10,000 agents ([Tumer & Holmes-Parker 2012](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~ktumer/publications/b2hd-tumer-holmesparker_ala12.html)). Those large-N results come from cheap closed-form utilities, though, not from re-running a full simulator, and the same group hit "severe computational issues" in a harder setting ([Curran et al. 2013](https://ifaamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2013/docs/p1281.pdf)). Dr.Reinforce is the closest published match to a critic-free version. It computes the policy-weighted difference directly on the true reward, avoiding the difficulty of learning COMA's Q-critic, at about |A_i| reward evaluations per agent per step. It beat COMA on Multi-Rover and Predator-Prey, but **its margin shrank as N grew from 3 to 8**, and it differences only the *immediate* reward ([Castellini et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2012.11258v2)). Here an upgrade or a routing choice pays off cycles later, so a one-cycle difference would be myopic.

The 2026 C3 method is the cleanest fork-based evidence. It resets to a recorded prefix, injects alternative actions, rolls the downstream agents to the terminal reward, and uses a leave-one-out baseline among the alternatives. With 4 continuations its advantage ranking agreed with a 16-continuation reference at **0.69** rank correlation, against **0.29** for a critic trained on the same rollouts, and it trained to MATH500 **82.8 vs 74.5 (MAGRPO) vs 69.3 (MAPPO)**. Its split-half reliability (agreement between advantages estimated from two independent halves of the data) fell from **0.64 on a 2-agent chain to 0.01 on a 10-agent chain**, because the per-agent signal shrank, not because the noise grew ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06859)). That is the central risk for 54-92 coupled agents: the per-agent effect may be tiny next to the team's. COSAC avoids extra simulation by fitting an additive per-agent decomposition with one ridge regression. At 16 agents its advantage error was about 2× lower than C3's and 30× lower than a shared group advantage, but it is tested only on one-pass bandits and assumes interactions are roughly additive ([Deshmukh et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.17693)). Shapley-value credit handles synergy but needs M·N coalition evaluations per credited decision, and every published variant evaluates them through a learned critic ([Li et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.00285)).

Cost decides the design. Let N be the agents credited, K the alternatives each, H the fork length in cycles and T the cycles credited per episode. Exact per-agent forks cost about T·N·K·H extra cycles per episode. With N = 92, K = 2, H = 5 and T = 100 that is about 92,000 cycles, **roughly 920 episodes of extra simulation per training episode** (arithmetic, not measured). Two facts make this tractable. First, each role shares one GNN, and the gradient pools all agents' samples, so an unbiased per-agent advantage on a *random subset* of agents and cycles should suffice. This is inferred, not tested in any source. Second, the 42-producer failure is concentrated in one decision type, upgrades. Forking only producer investment decisions, about 2 per episode with K = 2 and H = 30, adds roughly 1.2 episodes per episode. Two cautions carry over. C3 is exact only if "the trace is the state." The project already found that a deep-copied fork shares the routing tracer's closure and advances the live episode (DIAGNOSTICS 22), and the team potential tracker in `/srv/research/tokenomics/tokenomics/tokenomics/env/potential.py` wraps `System.complete_cycle` the same way, so forks must detach and re-attach it. And the default action must not trigger system death, or D_i measures "catastrophe avoided" rather than contribution.

Whether team-level credit already suffices is an empirical question with a cheap answer. MAPPO and IPPO train on a shared team reward and are strong on SMAC, Hanabi and GRF ([Yu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01955); [de Witt et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09533)). Both still use a learned value baseline, though, and their teams are small. Critic-free, a shared group advantage tied COSAC at 2 agents and fell behind at 10-16 ([Deshmukh et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.17693)). The project's agent count sits in the regime where difference rewards were invented. Parameter sharing pulls the other way. Measuring split-half gradient reliability at 12 and 42 producers settles which effect dominates before any fork code is written.

## Evolution strategies buy simplicity at 10-100× the episodes

**In plain terms.** Evolution strategies (ES) skip gradients through the policy altogether. They perturb the weights in many random directions, score each perturbed team on whole episodes, and move toward the directions that scored better. No critic and no credit assignment are needed, but the whole team's thousands of weights learn from one number per episode.

OpenAI-ES matched TRPO on MuJoCo with "no more than 10x" the data and solved 3D Humanoid in under 10 minutes, but only on **1,440 CPU workers**. One 18-core machine took about 11 hours ([Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.03864)). Its variance tricks, mirrored ±ε pairs and shared seeds, are cheap and directly usable. The closest multi-agent analogue trained one shared 70k-parameter policy for **50 UAVs** with 300 samples per iteration, over **two days on 244 hardware threads**, roughly 10⁴ thread-hours ([Fan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1803.01106v2)). Deep GA reached 4M parameters only with populations of 1,000-20,000 and about 10⁹ frames ([Such et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.06567)). Where pure evolutionary MARL appears as a baseline it is described as sample-inefficient ([ACE-MAPPO 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25091)). The successful multi-agent designs are hybrids that evolve teams on the team reward and take gradient steps on denser signals ([Khadka et al. 2020, MERL](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07315)). Full-covariance CMA-ES is out at d ≈ 2k-30k, and accelerator libraries such as EvoJAX help only when the simulator itself runs in JAX ([Tang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05008)), which this pure-Python domain does not.

The project's own record fixes the prior. Small ES residuals on frozen hand-written bases learned something but plateaued. The M5 producer residual gained +2-4% at 12 producers and stopped on the run-down clause after ≈ 65.2 vCPU-h. Earlier hard disqualification rules tied most ranks (37/48 and 42/48 members disqualified in EXP-0058), and the gradient-direction probe read `no_signal` until the base and rules changed (cosine 0.97 on k5_rf5, EXP-0086) ([HISTORY](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/HISTORY.md)). The binding constraint was **fitness noise and hard thresholds, not optimizer capacity**, and a few-thousand-parameter GNN makes the noise-per-dimension problem worse. A back-of-envelope cost at 5 s per 12-producer episode follows. A lean ES (32 antithetic pairs × 2 seeds = 128 episodes per update) costs about 0.18 vCPU-h per update, so 200 updates take **≈ 36 vCPU-h at 12 producers and ≈ 125 vCPU-h at 42** (assuming about 3.5× episode cost). Each update would see about 1% as many search directions as there are weights. Policy gradient, by contrast, extracts a training signal from every one of the ~6,200 agent decisions in an episode. ES earns a place only as a **low-dimensional search**, for example a few hundred parameters in an investment head on top of the frozen GNN, with CRN, a paired per-world fitness and smooth penalties. It is not the main learner.

## Centralized critics rarely travel across topologies

**In plain terms.** The drafted MAPPO uses a *centralized critic*: a network that looks at the whole economy and predicts how much the team will still earn. The fear is that a critic learned on some economies will be wrong on others. The literature says the fear is well founded for transfer, with one important qualification: in CTDE the critic is used only during training, so it needs to be right only on the worlds being trained on.

Value functions generalize worse than policies because they must encode facts the policy does not need. In IDAAC's example, two identical-looking starts have different values because one level is longer, so the critic "must memorize the number of remaining steps." In the same study, agents with *higher* value loss generalized better (Maze r = 0.96), and decoupling the value network from the policy gave the best Procgen test score (163.7) ([Raileanu & Fergus 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.10330)). PPO agents "strongly overfit" until they see about **10,000 levels** ([Cobbe et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01588)). The project has about 30 topologies. Units-to-go depends on cycles remaining, economy size and bottleneck structure, so negative held-out R² is the expected result, and EXP-0009 got exactly that for every model tried, including ridge regression, on 6 economies ([HISTORY §4](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/HISTORY.md)). EXP-0003's PPO critic, whose expected value sat at about 0, fits the second documented failure: unnormalized returns of very different scale. PopArt raised multi-task Atari from **59.7% to 110%** median human-normalized ([Hessel et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04474)), and MAPPO reports that value normalization "never hurts" and is "critical" in wide-range tasks ([Yu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01955)).

Architecture helps less than hoped. A permutation-invariant GNN critic improves training *at a fixed size* (PIC on up to 200 agents) but was never shown transferring zero-shot across sizes ([Liu et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00025)). MAT's zero-shot win rate on harder SMAC maps was **0% on 4 of 6** ([Wen et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14953)). On held-out roster sizes in RWARE, a fixed-width MAPPO critic scored **2.77 against 6.17** for critic-light IPPO, and a set critic recovered only to 5.67. The authors argue that a set-compatible critic "does not automatically provide the decentralized policy with a reusable notion of coordination" ([PC3D 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10377)). The transfer successes are **local, weight-shared policies**: a graph-RL router keeping about 97.8% of in-city performance zero-shot from New York to Shenzhen ([Gammelli et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/gammelli23a/gammelli23a.pdf)), and a critic-free GNN inventory policy whose optimality gap "remains flat" from 3 to 50 stores ([Alvo et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11246)). That favors the project's GNN actors and disfavors the planned pooled critic. UPDeT's pooled "aggregation" transformer underperformed a GRU ([Hu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.08001)), and global pooling is exactly where 12-versus-42 scale must be represented.

The qualification matters for the decision. If a critic is kept, it needs per-world normalized targets (the team potential already scales by s = H / B_we), explicit cycles-remaining and size inputs, no shared trunk with the actor, and all ~30 topologies including the 42s in training. Its health should be judged by **explained variance on the training worlds**, not held-out R². Even so, a critic is a learned approximation of a quantity this simulator can compute exactly by re-rolling the same seed. That is the core reason to go critic-free here.

## Recommendation: paired group-relative fine-tuning behind a champion gate

**In plain terms.** Start from the imitated GNN team, which already beats the best hand-written stack at 12 producers. On each training world, play the *same* seeded episode four times with the team sampling its actions. At every cycle, score each copy by how much more team potential it earned from that cycle on than the average of its three siblings, and nudge the policy toward the better copies. Keep the frozen champion as the referee: every so often, play candidate and champion on fresh identical worlds, and promote only on a statistically significant paired win. Before spending the training budget, run a short diagnostic that measures whether this signal is strong enough at 12 and at 42 producers, and whether the 42-producer drain needs per-decision forks.

The method has five parts, each tied to a diagnosed cause. **(1) Baseline: no critic.** The advantage for rollout i at cycle t is A_t^i = G_t^i − mean_{j≠i} G_t^j. Here G_t is the discounted return-to-go of the per-cycle team potential Φ_t (units minus the run-down, starvation and death terms, already scaled per world-episode in `env/potential.py`). It is mean-centered with no standard-deviation division (Dr. GRPO), and R = 4 rollouts share the world seed but differ in their policy draw keys. This addresses EXP-0009 and EXP-0003 (a critic that cannot be fitted across topologies or that stalls) and the user's portability concern directly: nothing is learned that must transfer. **(2) Per-cycle rather than per-episode comparison**, addressing the documented long-horizon dilution ([de Oliveira 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03527); [Wang 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19850)). The discount γ is set from the measured decorrelation horizon. **(3) An anchor to the imitation policy:** PPO-clip with a KL penalty to the imitated weights, as GRPO anchors to its reference, so RL refines a policy that is already +5.6% on held-out worlds instead of wandering from it ([EXP-0103](/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0103-m6-heldout/RESULT.md)). **(4) A champion gate after Kool:** every 15 iterations, a one-sided paired t-test at α = 0.05 on fresh train-split seeds (resampled after each swap), plus no extra deaths and run-down share no worse. This internal baseline swap is a training mechanic. Promotion of a project champion stays a human gate. **(5) Targeted fork credit for upgrades**, switched on only if Stage 0 shows the team signal misses them (below). The drafted `/srv/research/tokenomics/tokenomics/tokenomics/train/m6_mappo.py` already has the stochastic heads (`producer_dist`, `orderer_logp`), rollout jobs, the sharded synchronous update and keyed draws. The critic-free variant replaces `gae` and `CentralCritic` with the same-seed group baseline and adds the gate, a small code change.

One root-cause caveat comes first, under the project's "root cause before next strategy" rule. EXP-0103 recorded the 42-producer drain on 42-0125 as **"cause undiagnosed"**. The user characterizes it as the upgrade trap (DIAGNOSTICS 29). EXP-0104's drain tracer has run (`/srv/research/tokenomics/experiments/EXP-0104-m6-drain-42/artifacts/drain/summary.json`), but its `RESULT.md` is not yet written. Fork credit (part 5) is justified only once that record confirms upgrades past the sustainable maximum as the cause on non-held-out worlds. 42-0125 is a held-out world and must not be trained or tuned on.

Recomputing log-probabilities for about 400k decisions per iteration could cost up to as much as the rollouts, so one PPO epoch and the sharded update are assumed.

Over the pilot, the learner sees about 9,600 episodes, or roughly 60-80 million agent decisions, each with a same-seed per-cycle advantage. That is of the order of the 80M-transition budgets in which GR2PO came within 5-15% of PPO ([Wang 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19850)). Kool trained over 100 epochs from scratch, so whether 150 fine-tuning iterations suffice is the pilot's open question.

### Pros and cons

The case for it is that no learned quantity has to transfer across topologies. The baseline is exact for the world being trained on, and world difficulty cancels by construction. The approach has the strongest precedent in deterministic same-instance problems (Kool, POMO). It reuses the drafted MAPPO plumbing and existing fork infrastructure. The gate yields monotone, statistically protected progress and an automatic curriculum: the bar rises as the champion improves. Every advantage can be audited by replaying the seed. The costs are R× simulation per seed, credit that is still shared by all 54-92 agents (only temporal credit improves), and paired correlation that may decay quickly in a cascading economy, eroding the variance gain. There is also no published proof at this agent count and horizon. A further risk is that groups on saturated or collapsed worlds carry zero signal and waste a share of the budget, as the 52-54% constant-reward groups did in the paired-rollout study ([Sakib 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24144)). The main alternative's case should be stated fairly too. A per-world-normalized, decoupled critic trained on all ~30 topologies, 42s included, needs only to be useful on its own training worlds, and MAPPO is well tested on small teams. The project's two failed critics and the cross-size evidence make that a second-line bet, worth testing only if the critic audit in Stage 0 shows explained variance clearly above zero.

### Pre-registrable pilot design

**Question.** Does same-seed, per-cycle, leave-one-out policy-gradient fine-tuning of the imitated GNN team, with no critic, beat the frozen imitated champion on validation worlds at 12 producers without worsening side effects, and does it remove the run-down failure at 42?

**Stage 0 discriminating diagnostic** (decides whether and how Stage 1 runs). Five readings, pinned before any data:

- *D0.1, scenario share.* This is the fraction of episode-potential variance removed by grouping on seed, 1 − within-seed variance / total variance, per size.
- *D0.2, decorrelation horizon.* For sibling pairs on the same seed, this is the per-cycle Φ correlation as a function of cycles elapsed. t* is the first offset where the median drops below 0.5. It sets γ by the rule 1/(1−γ) ≈ t*, clipped to [0.9, 0.99].
- *D0.3, gradient reliability.* This is the split-half cosine between policy gradients estimated on two disjoint halves of the seeds, per role and size. It is computed for three estimators: E1, unpaired batch mean with episode return; E2, same-seed leave-one-out with episode return; E3, same-seed leave-one-out with per-cycle return-to-go. Each estimator's cosine is compared with a permutation null (advantages shuffled across seeds). Rule: Stage 1 uses the estimator with the highest cosine, and proceeds only if that cosine exceeds the null's 95th percentile at 12 producers for both roles. If it fails at both sizes, the result is `no_signal`: stop, record it, and change B or R only by a further pre-registration.
- *D0.4, upgrade credit.* This runs on non-held-out 42 worlds that EXP-0104 shows draining. At producer upgrade decisions, fork "as taken" against "upgrade suppressed" and play both to the horizon. Rule: if the forked advantage of upgrades past the sustainable maximum is negative in at least two thirds of forks, while E3's advantage at the same decisions agrees in sign in under half of them, Stage 1 adds forked credit on investment decisions; otherwise it does not. Forks must detach the potential and routing tracers (DIAGNOSTICS 22).
- *D0.5, critic audit* (reported, not deciding). Fit the drafted `CentralCritic` offline to per-world-normalized return-to-go from the Stage 0 rollouts, and report explained variance on unseen seeds of seen worlds and on unseen train worlds. This puts data under the portability question at no extra simulation cost.

Stage 0 also measures the 42-producer episode cost, which replaces the ×3.5 assumption in the Stage 1 budget.

**Stage 1 training.** Fixed in advance:

- Initialization: the EXP-0100 imitated models.
- Data: train split only, with fresh world-seeds every iteration. Groups with zero spread are dropped and their count is logged.
- Update: PPO-clip, 1 epoch, KL coefficient to the imitation policy starting at 0.04 (DeepSeekMath's value) with a pinned schedule; Adam, learning rate pinned.
- Gate: as in part (4) above.
- Stop rules: the 38 vCPU-h hard stop; early stop after 3 consecutive failed gates with a negative paired-gap trend.
- Logging, per iteration: the paired gap to the champion per size, the group-spread share, the gradient cosine and KL.

**Stage 2 deciding read.** Fixed in advance:

- Comparison: the last gated candidate against the frozen imitated champion and against k5_rf5 + MWWk, on the M6 validation set with the EXP-0102 rule.
- Deciding at 12 producers: `better` if mean units minus the champion exceeds the floor, deaths are not higher, and run-down and starvation shares are not worse.
- 42 producers, a pre-registered clause: the run-down share is not worse than the base's on the 42 validation worlds. This is the targeted cause.
- Outcomes:
  - `better` + 42 clause passes: the case for a held-out read via the ledger, a human gate.
  - `better` at 12 but 42 short: credit for the drain is not reached by team-level signal, which justifies D0.4-style forks or a low-dimensional ES head on investment, with the cause recorded.
  - `parity`, with D0.3 having passed: the signal exists but did not convert within budget. The next step is diagnosed by comparing iteration curves, not by guessing.

## Conclusion

The AlphaGo intuition transfers, but not in the form first imagined. What makes "beat the champion on the same game" powerful is not the opponent. It is that the comparison cancels everything except the policy difference. A deterministic, forkable simulator gives that cancellation more cleanly than Go ever did, because the "same game" can be replayed exactly, branched mid-episode and audited afterward. The champion belongs at the gate, and the learner's own siblings belong in the gradient. That split is the main design lesson the routing literature paid for.

The open risk has moved. It is no longer "can a critic generalize?", which the project can now sidestep, but "does a team-level signal reach individual agents and delayed investment decisions at 42 producers?" C3's reliability collapse over long agent chains and Dr.Reinforce's shrinking margin suggest it may not. That makes the 42-producer upgrade decisions the place where per-decision forks, rather than more data, decide success. Stage 0 measures exactly that before the budget is committed.
