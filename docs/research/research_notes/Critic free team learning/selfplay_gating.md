# Self-play, league, PBT and champion gating: what they provide, what they need, and whether "team vs frozen champion on identical seeded scenarios" has precedent without interaction

Scope note: research done 2026-11-06. Sources are primary papers (arXiv via ar5iv HTML renders, DeepMind pages) unless stated. Numbers quoted are those the papers report. Items I could not verify from a fetched source are in the Gaps sections, not the findings.

## 1. AlphaGo Zero / AlphaZero: self-play, the evaluator gate, value net + MCTS; which parts need two-player zero-sum?

### Takeaway
AlphaGo Zero's loop is (self-play by the current *best* player with MCTS) -> (train net on MCTS visit distributions + game outcome z) -> (gate: candidate replaces best only if it wins by a 55% margin). AlphaZero (2017/2018) *dropped the gate* and trained one continually updated network, so gating is a stability choice, not a requirement of the method. The zero-sum dependence sits in the outcome definition (z = win/loss from the mover's perspective), the minimax/alternating backup in MCTS, and the "opponent gets stronger as you do" curriculum; the gating step itself (candidate vs incumbent on a fixed match) is game-agnostic.

### Cited Findings
- AlphaGo Zero gate: the new player was compared to the current best; "if it won by a margin of 55% then it replaced the best player and self-play games were subsequently generated by this new player" — quoted in [Silver et al., AlphaZero preprint, arXiv 1712.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.01815) describing AlphaGo Zero ([Nature 2017, nature24270](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24270)).
- The gate match was 400 games at a 55% threshold — per reimplementations: ELF OpenGo "plays out 400 AI vs. AI games between the new model and the current model and accepts any new model with a 55% win rate or higher" ([ELF OpenGo doc](https://www.scribd.com/document/617566873/ELF-OpenGo)); the AlphaZero.jl docs state the original used 400 evaluation games with a 55% update threshold ([AlphaZero.jl docs](https://docs.juliahub.com/General/AlphaZero/stable/autodocs/)). (Secondary sources; the Nature text itself was paywalled to the fetcher.)
- AlphaZero removed the gate: it "simply maintains a single neural network that is updated continually, rather than waiting for an iteration to complete" — [arXiv 1712.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.01815).
- AlphaZero changed outcome modelling: AlphaGo Zero assumed binary win/loss; AlphaZero "estimates and optimises the expected outcome, taking account of draws or potentially other outcomes" — [arXiv 1712.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.01815).
- AlphaZero: 800 MCTS simulations per move in training; ~44M (chess), 24M (shogi), 21M (Go) self-play games; 700k steps, batch 4,096; same hyper-parameters across games except exploration noise; vs AlphaGo Zero (3-day) 60 wins / 40 losses — [arXiv 1712.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.01815).
- Gating remains common in open reimplementations: KataGo candidates must win at least 100 of 200 test games — [Wu, KataGo, arXiv 1902.10565](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.10565). ELF OpenGo (2,000 GPUs, 9 days) reports high variance in model strength during training, the usual argument for a gate — [Tian et al., ELF OpenGo, arXiv 1902.04522](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1902.04522).

### Inferences
- Parts that genuinely need two-player zero-sum: (a) the value target z in {-1,+1} defined relative to an opponent who is the same net; (b) MCTS backups that flip sign / alternate min-max between players; (c) the automatic curriculum ("opponent strength tracks your strength"), which exists because winning is defined against the opponent. Parts that do not: the policy-improvement operator (MCTS visit counts as policy targets), the value net as a state evaluator, and the gate (candidate vs incumbent on a fixed evaluation set). In a cooperative, non-interacting setting, (c) can be recreated by defining "win" = "my team beats the frozen champion's team on the same seeded world" — exactly what Ranked Reward / GAZ PTP / Kool do (sections 3, 5).
- The AlphaZero ablation-by-practice (dropping the gate and still converging) suggests the gate is mainly protection against regressions in a noisy evaluation, not the learning signal. For seeded deterministic worlds the evaluation noise comes only from world sampling and policy stochasticity, so a paired test is cheap and strong.

### Gaps
- Exact AlphaGo Zero numbers (1,600 simulations/move, 25,000 self-play games per checkpoint, 4.9M games over 3 days, Elo 5,185, 100-0 vs AlphaGo Lee) are from memory of the Nature paper; the Nature page redirected to a login and was not verified here. An Axios report says "roughly 5 million" self-play games ([Axios 2017](https://www.axios.com/new-alphago-ai-learns-without-help-from-humans-2497855313.html)).
- I found no published ablation that isolates the benefit of the AlphaGo Zero 55% gate vs continuous update in Go.

## 2. AlphaStar league, PSRO, fictitious self-play: what problem they solve; relevance without an adversary

### Takeaway
These methods exist to fight *non-transitivity and exploitability* in competitive games: naive self-play forgets, cycles (rock-paper-scissors), and overfits to its current opponent. They need an opponent whose policy changes the agent's payoff. In a cooperative economy with no adversary, the main surviving idea is PSRO's diagnosis of *overfitting to co-trained partners* (joint-policy correlation), which does apply inside a cooperative team.

### Cited Findings
- AlphaStar League = "main agents whose goal is to win versus everyone" + "exploiter agents that focus on helping the main agent grow stronger by exposing its flaws"; agents periodically add frozen copies of themselves to the league; it extends fictitious self-play ("playing against a mixture of all previous strategies"), which alone was insufficient — [DeepMind AlphaStar blog, 2019-10-30](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning/).
- Why plain self-play fails there: an agent "may forget how to win against a previous version of itself", "chasing its tail" cycles (rock-paper-scissors), and naive RL narrows onto easy-to-improve strategies — [DeepMind AlphaStar blog](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning/).
- AlphaStar started from imitation learning (initial policy beat 84% of active players); final result: above 99.8% of active Battle.net players, Grandmaster for all three races — [DeepMind AlphaStar blog](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning/); Nature paper: Vinyals et al. 2019, [nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1724-z](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1724-z).
- League composition per a later NeurIPS 2023 paper (ROA-Star): one main agent, one main exploiter, two league exploiters per race; exploiters lose the ability to find main-agent weaknesses in later iterations — [NeurIPS 2023 ROA-Star](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/94796017d01c5a171bdac520c199d9ed-Paper-Conference.pdf).
- PSRO (Lanctot et al., NeurIPS 2017): population per player + empirical payoff tensor + meta-solver (regret matching, Hedge, projected replicator dynamics) + deep-RL best-response oracle; generalizes independent RL, iterated best response, fictitious play and double oracle — [arXiv 1711.00832](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00832).
- PSRO's motivating problem: "policies learned using InRL can overfit to the other agents' policies during training". Joint-policy correlation (JPC) matrices (diagonal = trained together, off-diagonal = separate runs): in Laser Tag small2 an independent learner loses ~34.2% of reward when paired with another run's partner; DCH (level 10) cut the drop from 0.342->0.055, 0.625->0.082, 0.717->0.150 on small2/3/4. Gathering and pathfind showed no JPC problem, attributed to coordination not being required — [arXiv 1711.00832](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00832).

### Inferences
- With two non-interacting teams there is no payoff coupling, so there is no non-transitivity between "my team" and "the champion team": performance on a fixed world set is a scalar, rankings are transitive, and exploiters / meta-Nash solvers have nothing to exploit. The league machinery is not needed for the champion comparison.
- Non-transitivity can still arise *within* the team (shared producer policy vs shared orderer policy co-adapting): PSRO's JPC result says co-trained partners can overfit to each other's conventions. In this project that shows up as role-swap cross-play (new producer + champion orderer, etc.). The note-worthy precedent: JPC was large only where coordination mattered — and routing/production in the economy is a coordination problem.
- A "league of past champions" can be useful in a cooperative setting only as a *partner* pool (section 4, FCP), not as an opponent pool.

### Gaps
- Prioritized fictitious self-play (PFSP) weighting details from the AlphaStar Nature paper were not verifiable (paywalled; the blog does not mention PFSP).

## 3. PBT, champion/challenger, ratings as fitness, relative fitness in coevolution, Ranked Reward

### Takeaway
PBT needs no opponent: fitness is any task metric (mean of last 10 episode rewards in RL). Ranked Reward (R2) is the closest precedent for turning single-agent optimization into self-play: reward is +1/-1 depending on whether this episode beats a percentile of the agent's own recent episodes. It is a *population-free, opponent-free* curriculum that keeps the bar rising. Coevolution's "relative fitness" literature warns that in cooperative coevolution, judging a component by its average performance with partners leads to *relative overgeneralization* (robust-but-mediocre solutions).

### Cited Findings
- PBT (Jaderberg et al. 2017): exploit = copy weights+hyperparameters of a better member (truncation selection: bottom 20% copy a uniformly sampled top-20% member; binary tournament; or a Welch t-test on the last 10 episodic rewards); explore = multiply hyperparameters by 0.8 or 1.2 (2.0/0.5 for GANs); RL members "ready" every 1e6-1e7 steps — [arXiv 1711.09846](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.09846).
- PBT fitness in RL = mean of last 10 episodic rewards; MT = BLEU; GAN = Inception-score variant. No opponent is required — [arXiv 1711.09846](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.09846).
- PBT results: DM Lab (UNREAL, pop 40) 93% -> 106% human-normalised; Atari (FuN, pop 80) 147% -> 181% on four games; StarCraft II (A3C, pop 30) 36% -> 39%; Transformer MT (pop 32) BLEU 22.30 -> 22.65; populations of 20-40 give strong gains with diminishing returns beyond — [arXiv 1711.09846](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.09846).
- Ranked Reward (Laterre et al., NeurIPS 2018 Deep RL workshop): motivated by self-play working for two-player games but not single-player combinatorial optimization — [arXiv 1807.01672](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01672).
- R2 mechanism: buffer of the 250 most recent rewards; threshold r_alpha = buffer reward closest to the alpha-th percentile; z = +1 if r > r_alpha (or r = 1, optimal), -1 if r < r_alpha, random ±1 if r = r_alpha < 1 ("used to break ties and assure constant-learning"); alpha = 50% is equivalent to playing an equal-strength opponent. MCTS 300 simulations/move, AlphaZero-style policy+value net — [arXiv 1807.01672 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1807.01672).
- R2 results on 2D/3D bin packing: beats plain MCTS, the Lego heuristic, a supervised agent and Gurobi (5 min, 8 CPUs); R2-75% beat Gurobi by "more than 6%", up to 15% at larger sizes. 3D, 10 items, networks without MCTS: rank-free 0.807 vs rank-50% 0.902, rank-75% 0.903, rank-90% 0.871 — [ar5iv 1807.01672](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1807.01672).
- R2 threshold dynamics: 50% converges slowly (rewards many suboptimal games), 75% filters best, 90% unstable; rank-50% generalized better at test despite 75% training better — [ar5iv 1807.01672](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1807.01672).
- Cooperative coevolution: relative overgeneralization = populations settle on solutions that work well with many partners rather than the optimum; shown by Wiegand when individuals are evaluated on part of the problem; Panait/Wiegand/Luke (IJCAI 2003) bias evaluation toward best-partner collaboration; Bucci & Pollack (GECCO 2005) add Pareto dominance — [Bucci & Pollack GECCO 2005](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gecco2005/docs/p539.pdf); [Panait et al. GECCO 2004](https://cs.york.ac.uk/rts/docs/GECCO_2004/Conference%20proceedings/papers/3102/31020573.pdf).

### Inferences
- R2's binary win/loss against a self-derived threshold normalizes scale across instances of different difficulty — directly useful when worlds differ in achievable consumer units (12 vs 42 producers, tight vs loose). But R2 compares against *other instances'* rewards (a buffer), which mixes world difficulty into the signal; a *paired, same-seed* comparison against a frozen champion (section 5) removes that confound and is strictly better suited to seeded deterministic worlds.
- Elo/TrueSkill/Bradley-Terry exist to aggregate non-transitive or noisy pairwise outcomes among many players. With two non-interacting teams on fixed seeds, a paired difference (or a sign test / paired t-test, as Kool uses) is the natural statistic; ratings add nothing unless a population of many champions is kept.
- PBT is orthogonal and compatible: it can run on top of any of these signals; its own t-test exploit rule is another precedent for statistically gated replacement.
- The relative-overgeneralization warning applies if producer and orderer policies are scored by average performance across varied partners; scoring the joint team (both shared policies together) against a fixed champion team avoids it.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch a primary source for Elo/TrueSkill use as RL fitness (e.g. AlphaStar's league uses Elo-like matchmaking); not needed for non-interacting teams but left uncited.
- Rosin & Belew (1997) competitive-coevolution "relative fitness" / hall of fame not fetched.

## 4. Cooperative self-play: Other-Play, Fictitious Co-Play, Hanabi, Overcooked

### Takeaway
In cooperative games "self-play" means training all seats with the same (or co-evolving) policy; it reliably reaches high self-play scores but learns arbitrary conventions that break with any other partner (cross-play). Other-Play and FCP fix *ad-hoc partner* robustness. They do not provide a curriculum or opponent; the relevance here is limited to the case where a new learned role is deployed next to a different (e.g. champion or hand-written) partner role.

### Cited Findings
- Other-Play (Hu, Lerer, Peysakhovich, Foerster, ICML 2020): SP agents coordinate on how to break symmetries during training and fail with independently trained partners; OP trains against partners relabelled by random symmetry permutations; zero-shot coordination = coordinate with novel partners not seen before — [arXiv 2003.02979](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979).
- Hanabi (out of 25): SAD self-play 23.97 vs cross-play 2.52; SAD+OP cross-play 15.32; SAD+AUX+OP cross-play 22.07 with self-play 24.06. With humans: OP bot 15.75 vs SP bot 9.15 (bomb-out 45% vs 85%) — [arXiv 2003.02979](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979).
- Lever game: SP picks one of the nine 1.0 levers and gets ~0.11 in cross-play; OP picks the unique 0.9 lever — [arXiv 2003.02979](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979).
- Fictitious Co-Play (Strouse et al., NeurIPS 2021): train N=32 SP agents (different seeds), keep 3 checkpoints each (init, 50% of final reward, final), then train one agent as best response to this frozen pool. Overcooked deliveries vs held-out partners: Hproxy 10.6 (FCP) vs 4.7 (no checkpoints); random partner 8.6 vs 1.0. Population N=4 -> 8.4, N=32 -> 10.6 (plateau). Human study N=114: FCP beats SP (t=12.3), PP (8.2), BCP (7.8), all p<0.001 — [arXiv 2110.08176](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.08176).
- FCP diagnosis: SP agents "only ever need to coordinate with themselves"; population play in common-payoff games "naturally encourages agents to play the same way" — [arXiv 2110.08176](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.08176).

### Inferences
- When the whole team (all producers + all orderers via two shared policies) is always deployed together, cross-play robustness is not the objective; self-play conventions are fine. The project's role-swap measurements (new producer with a different orderer) are cross-play and would inherit the SP brittleness documented here — a reason to keep candidate-vs-champion comparisons at the *whole-team* level.
- FCP is the cooperative analogue of a "league of frozen past checkpoints": past snapshots are used as *partners* to train a robust best response. That is distinct from the champion-as-baseline idea; it would only matter if mixed teams (learned + hand-written roles) are deployed.

### Gaps
- No cooperative-self-play paper found that uses a frozen past team as a *comparison baseline on identical scenarios* (as opposed to a partner). That precedent comes from combinatorial optimization (section 5).

## 5. Paired comparison against a baseline/champion policy on the same scenarios as the learning signal (Kool, POMO, GAZ PTP) — precedent for "team vs frozen champion, no interaction"

### Takeaway
Strong, direct precedent exists in neural combinatorial optimization, a single-agent, non-adversarial setting. Kool et al. (ICLR 2019) use the greedy rollout of the *best model so far* on the *same instance* as the REINFORCE baseline and replace it only after a one-sided paired t-test (alpha = 5%, 10,000 instances) — i.e. a champion with statistical gating, explicitly framed as self-play-like. GAZ PTP (Pirnay et al. 2023) makes it literal: the learner "plays" a frozen historical policy on identical copies of the instance, binary win if its return is at least as good, champion replaced via an arena set. POMO shows the main weakness of a champion baseline (advantages mostly negative, higher variance) and replaces it with a same-instance multi-rollout mean. The two "teams" never interact in any of these — only their outcomes on the same instance are compared.

### Cited Findings
- Kool, van Hoof, Welling, "Attention, Learn to Solve Routing Problems!" (ICLR 2019): baseline b(s) = "the cost of a solution from a deterministic greedy rollout of the policy defined by the best model so far"; frozen per epoch like a DQN target network — [arXiv 1803.08475](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.08475).
- Kool gate: at each epoch end compare current (greedy) vs baseline policy; replace baseline only if a one-sided paired t-test is significant at alpha = 5% on 10,000 separate evaluation instances; resample evaluation instances after each replacement to avoid overfitting — [arXiv 1803.08475](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.08475).
- Kool on self-play: notes "similarities with self-play improvement" (Silver et al. 2017): sampling replaces tree search for exploration, the model is rewarded for beating the best model so far, and is "always challenged by the best model", as in AlphaGo — [arXiv 1803.08475](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.08475).
- Kool baseline comparison (TSP20, gap vs Gurobi, two seeds): rollout 0.34%/0.29%, exponential (beta=0.8) 0.95%/0.93%, critic 0.96%/0.97%; rollout "improves the quality and convergence speed" and is "much more effective" than the critic; exponential ~20% faster per epoch, critic ~13% slower than rollout. Exponential baseline used during the first epoch as warmup. Training 100 epochs x 2,500 batches x 512 instances. Greedy gaps TSP20/50/100: 0.34%/1.76%/4.53% — [arXiv 1803.08475](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.08475).
- POMO (Kwon et al., NeurIPS 2020): N rollouts per instance from different start nodes; shared baseline = mean reward of the N rollouts on the same instance; advantages are zero-mean, whereas greedy-rollout advantages are "usually negative" because samples rarely beat the greedy rollout; lower gradient variance; no cloned baseline network; "highly resistant to local minima". TSP100 gap: AM greedy 3.51% vs POMO single trajectory 1.07%, POMO x8 augmentation 0.14%; CVRP100 6.72% -> 0.32% (vs LKH3) — [arXiv 2010.16011](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.16011).
- GAZ PTP, "Policy-Based Self-Competition for Planning Problems" (Pirnay, Göttl, Burger, Grimm; arXiv 2306.04403, 2023, ICLR 2023 per my recollection — venue not shown on the fetched page): single-player problem reframed as a two-player game; both players start from identical copies of the instance; opponent = frozen historical policy acting greedily (or sampled); learner wins if its return is at least as good (ties to learner); value net trained on ±1; historical policy replaced when current policy's summed greedy outcome beats it on a fixed arena set; self-play probability gamma = 0.2 for stability early — [arXiv 2306.04403](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.04403).
- GAZ PTP results: TSP n=100 gap 3.16% vs 27.15% (single-player GAZ) and 44.43% (greedy scalar baseline); TSP n=50 1.55% vs 6.36%; JSSP 30x20 30.6% vs 34.4%; on JSSP 15x15 the greedy-scalar baseline (17.8%) was competitive with PTP (16.6-18.4%), so gains are not uniform. Caveat: improved search does not always yield an improved greedy policy, which can delay champion updates — [arXiv 2306.04403](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.04403).

### Inferences
- "Team vs frozen champion on identical seeded worlds, teams do not interact" has clear precedent: it is Kool's rollout baseline (continuous paired advantage + t-test gate), GAZ PTP's self-competition (binary paired win + arena gate), and AlphaGo Zero's evaluator (gate only). None requires interaction or zero-sum structure; the "opponent" only supplies a same-instance reference outcome.
- What it provides in this project's setting: (a) a per-world, per-seed baseline that cancels world difficulty (huge variance reduction when worlds differ by orders of magnitude in consumer units); (b) an automatic curriculum — the bar is the best team so far, so reward stays informative as the team improves (R2/PTP argument); (c) monotone, statistically gated champion promotion (Kool t-test, AlphaGo Zero 55%, KataGo 100/200), protecting against regressions; (d) critic-free learning signal — Kool found the rollout baseline beat a learned critic, relevant for a "critic-free team learning" design.
- What it does not provide: per-agent credit assignment. The paired advantage is a single team-level scalar per world-episode; with 12-42 producers and ~50 orderers each agent's gradient still carries all others' noise. Counterfactual or per-agent baselines would be needed separately.
- Known failure modes to plan for: advantages mostly negative once the champion is strong (POMO critique) — consider a same-world multi-rollout mean (POMO style, N sampled team rollouts per seed) instead of or alongside the champion; binary win signals lose magnitude information and need tie handling (R2's random sign; PTP's ties-to-learner); champion promotion can stall if the evaluated (greedy/deterministic) policy lags the sampled one (PTP caveat); evaluation-set overfitting (Kool resamples evaluation instances after each promotion).
- Deterministic seeded worlds make the paired test unusually clean: the only noise left is policy sampling, so a sign test or paired t-test over seeds has high power at small N.

### Gaps
- Self-critical sequence training (Rennie et al. 2017, greedy decode of the current policy as baseline) and Kool et al. 2019 "Buy 4 REINFORCE samples, get a baseline for free" (leave-one-out multi-sample baseline) are directly relevant precedents but were not fetched within the tool budget.
- I found no published *multi-agent cooperative* RL work (MAPPO/QMIX family) that uses a frozen champion team's same-seed outcome as the advantage baseline; the precedent is single-agent CO and two-player games. This may exist under other names (e.g. "self-competition", "baseline policy rollout") and was not exhaustively searched.
